Bumpr: Post Mortem

Executive Summary

  • What: a difficult, mouse-only precision platformer
  • Scope: built solo in roughly 90 hours; released on Itch.io in September 2026
  • Unique selling point: mouse-driven movement as the core mechanic, paired with a steep learning curve
    • Sets Bumpr apart from the many D-pad platformers in a saturated, highly competitive market

What Went Right vs. What Went Wrong

Scope & Design

What Went Right

  • Focused on refining a single core mechanic
    • Other mechanics were included only if they interacted with movement in a unique way and were fun. Examples:
      • Kept: breakable walls added complexity to movement and level design and felt satisfying
        • Players slam into them repeatedly
      • Scrapped: the “Sleepers” (purple enemies)
        • Forcing players to sneak past them broke the pacing
        • Conflicted with the goal of finishing fast for a high rank
        • User feedback indicated it wasn’t fun

What Went Wrong

  • Too many mechanics were introduced without an on-ramp
  • Most common feedback: the difficulty curve spiked too quickly and checkpoints were too sparse
  • Hard to tell whether frustration came from well-balanced levels or from poor UX

Scope Control

What Went Right

  • Defined a written MVP and checked every new idea against it
  • Kept an anti-feature list to stay focused on the core experience
  • Parked big ideas (e.g., harder level variations) in a backlog
  • Treated the project as explicitly not a passion project
    • Made it easier to cut ideas I’d grown attached to

What Went Wrong

  • Over-engineered a modular state machine to track player states by movement speed
    • One or two simple scripts would have done
    • Built on a “what if I need it later?” mindset; spent days on a system that was never used

Architecture

What Went Right

  • ScriptableObject GameEvent architecture, adopted early
    • Gave decoupled code and flexible runtime communication
  • Object pooling for audio, particles and projectiles
    • Removed stutter during rapid wall bounces
  • ScriptableObjects for level data
    • Made it easy to tune par times from playtest data

What Went Wrong

  • Large stretches of programming-only work made progress feel intangible and stifled momentum

Process & Motivation

What Went Right

  • Trello board with clear “next step” tasks and short-timeframe milestones
    • Kept progress visible and motivation steady

What Went Wrong

  • Self-doubt and perfectionism led to bursts of productivity followed by waves of procrastination
  • Almost every area (sprite tiling, animation, audio) was new to me
    • Great for learning, but it created friction and overwhelm

Art & Audio

What Went Right

  • First-pass audio and sprites were highly motivating and made progress tangible

What Went Wrong

  • Added art and placeholder audio too late

Marketing & Reach

What Went Right

  • N/A

What Went Wrong

  • Neglected marketing: devlogs, short-form content and streamer outreach are invaluable in a space that depends on virality
  • Playtesting, outreach and accessibility were also neglected
    • Accessibility was rushed at the end

Key Takeaways

If I started again, I would:

  1. Make it work first, even if it’s hardcoded.
    • Over-engineering isn’t worth it if the core loop turns out not to be fun
  2. Iterate on art and sound throughout.
    • Visible progress is vital for staying motivated
  3. Invest more in marketing (Shorts, TikTok, Discord, Reddit, etc.).
    • Virality is one of the most effective ways to gain exposure
